Emergency Leak Detection in Wilmington, NC

A leak you can't see is still a leak you're paying for. We find it fast, without guessing and without digging up the whole yard to do it.

If your water bill jumped for no reason, or there's a warm patch on the floor near the water heater closet, something is leaking somewhere you can't see it. Leak detection in Wilmington, NC means finding that exact spot before it turns into a bigger repair, whether the leak is under a slab, inside a wall, or running along a buried service line.

Homeowners usually call us for one of two reasons: a bill that's climbing with no obvious cause, or a physical sign like a soft floor, a musty smell, or a hissing sound near a wall. Both are worth a same-day look, because a slow leak under a slab foundation, common in New Hanover County's newer subdivisions, rarely stays slow for long.

If the leak is coming directly from the tank itself rather than a supply line, that's usually a water heater repair call instead, and we'll point you the right direction on the phone.

Emergency response

Emergency leak detection when water is already moving

Some leaks can wait for a daytime appointment. Others cannot. If water is coming through a ceiling, soaking a wall, pooling in a crawl space, or running into a ceiling light fixture, the damage grows by the hour. Call the emergency line and we dispatch a plumber with leak location equipment.

The first goal is to stop the water. The second is to find the exact source without opening more of your home than necessary. We use acoustic listening gear and pressure testing to narrow the leak to a small area before anyone cuts into drywall or concrete.

Why this happens in Wilmington

Why hidden leaks are common in Wilmington homes

Wilmington sits on sandy coastal soil with pockets of Piedmont clay. That soil expands and contracts with seasonal rainfall, and the movement puts real stress on pipe joints running beneath slab and crawl-space foundations. A joint that was solid when the house was built can work loose over ten or fifteen years of that shifting. The first sign is usually a leak, not a visible crack.

The area's humid subtropical climate adds a second factor: constant moisture and salt-laden coastal air accelerate corrosion on older galvanized and copper supply lines faster than in a drier inland climate. That's part of why PEX has become the more common repair material here, since it resists the corrosion pattern that eventually pinholes an aging copper line.

Signs to watch for

Signs you have a hidden leak

  • Water bill increased noticeably with no change in household water use.
  • A warm spot on the floor, especially near a water heater closet or garage slab edge.
  • The water meter dial keeps moving even with every fixture in the house shut off.
  • A damp or musty smell in one room with no visible source.
  • A hissing or trickling sound inside a wall or under the floor.
  • Unexplained mold or a soft, spongy patch on drywall near a plumbing wall.

Water bill spiking or a meter that will not stop

One of the most common reasons people call about a leak is the meter. Water use jumps on the bill, or the meter keeps turning when every tap, appliance, and irrigation zone is off. That pattern usually means water is escaping somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

We start by closing the main valve at the house and watching whether the meter still moves. If it stops, the leak is inside. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the line between the meter and the house. That one test saves hours of guessing. Read our guide to reading your water bill for leak signs if you want to check first.

Water coming through a ceiling or wall

A stain spreading across a ceiling, a bubble in the paint, or a wall that feels damp is a leak that has already traveled. The wet spot is rarely where the leak starts. Water follows framing and pipes before it shows up, and attic leaks can run a long way across insulation first.

We trace the stain back along supply lines, drains, and fixtures above it. A moisture meter and pressure test tell us whether the source is a supply line, a drain joint, a shower pan, or the roof. If the roof is the real source, we will say so rather than open walls for nothing.

Leaks under the house and in the crawl space

Wilmington homes often sit on raised foundations with plumbing exposed under the floor. A dripping joint, a wet insulation batt, or a musty smell from the crawl space can mean a supply or drain leak that has gone on for weeks. Dark, damp soil under one spot is a strong clue.

We inspect the lines from underneath, listen to pressurized pipes, and check insulation and vapor barriers for staining. Our post on crawl space moisture in Wilmington explains how plumbing leaks and ground moisture get confused.

Irrigation, yard, and meter box leaks

A soggy patch of lawn that never dries, a pool of water near the street, or a meter box that stays full usually points to a buried line, an irrigation valve, or a connection at the meter. Pool and spa supply lines can leak the same way.

We shut down irrigation zones one at a time and check the service line to separate a sprinkler leak from a house supply leak. If the leak is on the utility side of the meter, we tell you who to call instead of billing you to find it.

If you're not sure it's a "leak detection" problem

Most people who call us don't use the phrase "leak detection" at all. They say the meter is still running with the water off, or the water bill went up and they don't know why, or there's a warm spot on the floor near the garage. All three of those are exactly what leak detection equipment is built to solve.

The short version: if you can't see where water is coming from but you're confident it's going somewhere, that's a detection job, not a guess-and-cut repair. If instead the water is backing up rather than disappearing, that's more likely a drain cleaning issue than a leak. We locate the leak first, then talk through the actual fix.

How we find and fix it

How we actually find the leak

We start by isolating whether the leak is on the supply side or the drain side. The water meter is the first test: if the dial moves with every fixture off, the leak is in the supply line somewhere between the meter and the house, or inside the home's plumbing itself.

From there we use acoustic listening equipment to trace pipe sound along the suspected run, and thermal imaging to spot a temperature difference in flooring or drywall that a hot-water leak often creates. For a suspected slab leak, this combination usually narrows the location to a small, specific area rather than a general room.

Only once the leak is pinpointed do we talk about access. In most cases that means a small, targeted opening rather than tearing out an entire slab section or wall, which keeps both the disruption and the repair cost down.

Homeowner's insurance in North Carolina typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from a burst pipe but not the slow, ongoing leak itself or general wear-and-tear corrosion, so document the damage with photos and check your specific policy before assuming coverage.

If a home has had more than one leak in different spots over the past year or two, the underlying pipe material itself may be the real problem, not each leak individually. In that case, whole-home repiping is usually the more honest recommendation than another round of spot repairs.
