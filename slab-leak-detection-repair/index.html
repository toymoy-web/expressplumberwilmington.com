Emergency Slab Leak Detection & Repair in Wilmington, NC

A warm patch on the floor, a hardwood plank that's cupping, a water bill that keeps climbing. A leak under a concrete slab needs different equipment than any other leak in the house, and we bring exactly that.

Slab leak detection and repair in Wilmington, NC is a more specific version of general leak detection, focused entirely on pipes running under a concrete slab foundation. The diagnostic approach and the repair options are genuinely different from a leak inside a wall or crawl space, which is why this gets its own dedicated process rather than a generic leak call.

Most slab leak calls start with one of two signs. A warm spot on an otherwise cool floor usually means a hot water line, while a cold spot paired with a rising water bill more often points to a cold water line. Both are worth acting on quickly, since water under a slab has nowhere to go but up, into flooring and eventually framing.

Emergency response

Emergency slab leak response, day or night

A slab leak can start small and turn urgent fast. A warm patch on the floor, the sound of running water with every fixture off, or a meter that keeps turning all point to a line failing under the concrete. Hot water lines are the most common culprit, and they can waste water and gas or electricity at the same time.

Call the emergency line if the floor is wet, the spot is hot to the touch, or water pressure has dropped sharply. We isolate the line, locate the leak with acoustic equipment, and give you the repair options and a flat price before any concrete is opened.

Before we arrive: what to do about a possible slab leak

  • Shut off the main water valve to stop the flow while you wait.
  • Turn off the water heater at the breaker or gas control if the leak is on a hot line, so the tank does not run dry.
  • Keep children and pets off any warm or wet floor and move rugs and furniture away.

Why this happens in Wilmington

Why slab leaks happen in Wilmington specifically

Wilmington's sandy coastal soil shifts more than a stable clay base would as seasonal moisture changes. That movement stresses the pipe joints running through and under a slab in exactly the spot they're hardest to inspect or maintain, which is why slab leaks show up here more than in a geologically stable inland market.

Homes built from the 1990s onward in the newer subdivisions further from downtown are more likely to sit on slab foundations in the first place, since slab construction became more common in residential building during that period. That's part of why slab leak calls skew toward these newer neighborhoods rather than the older, often crawl-space-foundation homes closer to downtown.

Signs to watch for

Signs of a slab leak

  • A warm spot on the floor with no obvious source, especially over a hot water line's likely path.
  • Hardwood or laminate flooring cupping, buckling, or separating in one area.
  • A water bill that's risen with no change in household usage.
  • The sound of running water when every fixture in the house is off.
  • Unexplained mold or musty smell rising from the floor.

What a slab leak sounds and feels like

People describe slab leaks in a few consistent ways. A hot spot on a floor, the sound of water running when nothing is on, a bill that jumps, or a sudden drop in pressure. In rental homes and small apartment buildings, a tenant often reports a wet floor before the owner notices anything.

Property managers and owners with several units should call as soon as a floor feels warm or damp. A leak under one unit can affect shared lines, and acoustic location lets us find the problem without opening floors in every unit.

How we find and fix it

How we find a slab leak specifically

We start the same way as general leak detection, isolating supply side from drain side using the water meter, but the equipment gets more specific from there. Thermal imaging is especially useful for a suspected hot water line, since the leak often creates a detectable warm zone on the slab surface above it.

Acoustic listening equipment traces pipe sound through the slab itself, which behaves differently than sound traveling through open wall cavities, and takes real experience to interpret correctly. A slab leak's exact location is rarely obvious from the surface sign alone. The warm spot or wet flooring often sits some distance from the actual leak point.

Once located, we discuss access options before doing anything, since slab access is inherently more involved than opening a wall.
