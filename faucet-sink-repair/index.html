Emergency Faucet & Sink Repair in Wilmington, NC

A dripping faucet wastes more water than it looks like, and a leaking supply valve under the sink can quietly damage cabinetry for months before anyone notices. We fix both fast.

Faucet and sink repair in Wilmington, NC covers dripping or leaking faucets, worn cartridges, corroded supply valves under the sink, and full fixture replacement when a faucet is simply worn out or being upgraded during a renovation. Corroded valves are part of the same coastal-corrosion pattern described on our repiping page, just at a smaller scale.

Most drips trace back to a worn cartridge or O-ring inside the faucet body, a normal wear item on any faucet used daily for years. A leak under the sink is usually the shutoff valve or the supply line connection, a separate issue from the faucet itself.

Emergency response

Emergency faucet and valve repair for leaks that will not stop

Most faucet problems are slow drips. A few are real emergencies. A supply valve that snaps off, a faucet that sprays when it should be closed, or a braided supply line that bursts under the sink can flood a kitchen or bathroom in minutes.

Call the emergency line if the water will not stop. We walk you through finding the fixture shutoff or the main valve, then send a plumber to replace the valve, line, or faucet and test everything before leaving.

Before we arrive: what to do about a leaking faucet or valve

  • Turn the small shutoff valve under the sink or behind the fixture clockwise to stop the supply.
  • If that valve is stuck or broken, shut off the main water valve to the house.
  • Empty the cabinet under the sink and mop up standing water to protect the cabinet floor.
  • Call (919) 944-7281 and tell us which fixture is leaking and whether the water is off.
Why this happens in Wilmington

Why supply valves corrode faster here

The humid, salt-laden coastal air that affects larger supply lines across Wilmington affects the small shutoff valves under sinks too. That's especially true in homes near the coast, where that air infiltrates cabinets more than it would inland. A valve that's rarely turned can seize or start weeping years before it would in a drier climate.

This is also why a shutoff valve that hasn't been touched since the house was built is worth checking during any other sink work. A corroded valve you discover during an actual emergency, when you need to shut the water off fast, is a worse time to find out it doesn't turn.

Signs to watch for

Signs of a faucet or sink problem

  • A faucet drips steadily, even when fully closed.
  • Water pressure at one sink is noticeably lower than the rest of the house.
  • A puddle or water stains inside the cabinet under the sink.
  • The shutoff valve under a sink is stiff, seized, or won't fully close.
  • A faucet handle feels loose or requires more force to operate than it used to.

Valves, cartridges, and fixtures we are asked to repair

Most faucet calls come down to a worn cartridge, a failed washer, a corroded shutoff valve, or a loose supply line. Shower valves and tub faucets follow the same pattern, with leaks at the handle, spout, or showerhead.

We identify the brand and cartridge, then repair or replace the part. If parts are no longer made, we match style with a new fixture. A shutoff valve that will not close is replaced so the next repair is easy.

A broken or stuck supply valve

A supply valve that leaks at the stem, snaps when turned, or will not shut off is more than an annoyance. It can stop you from isolating a fixture, and a forced valve can fail and flood the cabinet.

We replace failed valves with new quarter turn valves and test every connection. If the valve will not close, we shut the water off at the main and replace it safely.

How we find and fix it

How we handle it

For a dripping faucet, we identify the faucet type first, compression, cartridge, ball, or ceramic disc, since each uses a different internal repair kit. Most drips are resolved with a cartridge or O-ring swap rather than a full faucet replacement.

For a leak under the sink, we trace it to the actual source before touching anything: the supply line connection, the shutoff valve itself, or the faucet's own supply nuts. Tightening the wrong connection is a common reason a "fixed" under-sink leak comes back.

If a shutoff valve is corroded enough that it won't reliably close, we replace it as part of the visit rather than leave a valve you can't trust in an emergency.
